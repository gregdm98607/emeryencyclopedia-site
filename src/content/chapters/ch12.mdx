# Chapter 12: Rock Art & Sacred Landscapes

In the summer of 1995 an art conservator spent six weeks on a scaffold in Buckhorn Wash, working inch by inch across a sandstone wall more than a hundred feet long. Beneath decades of chalk, charcoal, bullet scars, and spray-painted names lay a procession of red, elongated figures that had been painted there, by the best current estimates, somewhere between one and four thousand years earlier. When the scaffold came down that September and the county unveiled the cleaned panel as its contribution to Utah's statehood centennial, the figures were visible again in something close to the state their makers intended (Deseret News, 3 Sept. 1995). That moment captures the chapter that follows: Emery County holds one of the most concentrated collections of Indigenous rock art in North America, it is fragile, and what survives depends on people choosing to protect it.

From the life-sized spirit beings of the Barrier Canyon Style to the hunting scenes and ornamented figures of the Fremont and the historic-period images left by Ute people, these panels span thousands of years of human presence. Hundreds of documented panels lie in the San Rafael Swell and the canyons draining it, ranging from single figures to compositions more than 130 feet long (BLM, Buckhorn Panel; KSL, Buckhorn Wash). This chapter examines the styles, meanings, chronology, and preservation of Emery County's rock art while centering the perspectives and stewardship of the Ute, Paiute, Shoshone, and Puebloan peoples for whom these landscapes remain culturally alive.

## 12.1 Rock Art Styles: Barrier Canyon, Fremont, and Ute Traditions

Emery County's rock art encompasses three major cultural traditions, each distinct in style, period, and probable meaning.

### Barrier Canyon Style

The Barrier Canyon Style (BCS) — a classification established by archaeologist Polly Schaafsma in her 1971 study of the Donald Scott collection of Utah rock art — is the oldest and most enigmatic tradition in the county (Schaafsma 1971). It takes its name from Barrier Canyon, the older name for Horseshoe Canyon, whose Great Gallery lies just south of the county line in the Horseshoe Canyon unit of Canyonlands National Park. BCS sites cluster in the San Rafael Swell and adjacent drainages, where they appear mostly as pictographs (painted images) rather than petroglyphs (pecked or carved images). The style is characterized by life-sized or larger anthropomorphic figures: tall, slender, armless or nearly armless beings with elongated bodies, minimal facial features, and sometimes radiating lines, antennae, or crown-like extensions from the head. Many figures appear to float, and their postures often suggest movement or trance. Pigments are typically red ochre, though black and white appear in some panels (Schaafsma 1980; Cole 2009).

The age of the Barrier Canyon Style is unsettled, and the chapter presents the disagreement rather than resolving it. Schaafsma and most later writers assigned the style to the Archaic period, roughly 1,500 to 4,000 years ago, on the basis of style, associated artifacts, and a small number of direct radiocarbon dates on pigment (Schaafsma 1980; Cole 2009). A 2014 luminescence study of the Great Gallery, however, concluded that the panel's sandstone face was exposed by rockfall too recently for the earliest of those dates to hold, and constrained painting of that panel to roughly AD 1 to 1100 — the late Archaic to Fremont transition rather than the deep Archaic (Pederson et al. 2014). That result was challenged and defended in the same journal (Simon and Reed 2014; Pederson et al. 2015), and it applies directly only to the one panel tested. Readers should treat any single date for the style as provisional.

Interpretation is likewise open. Many researchers, including Schaafsma, have proposed that the figures represent shamans in altered states, spirit beings, or visionary experience central to Archaic religious practice, drawing on ethnographic accounts of hunter-gatherer religion across the Great Basin and far western North America (Schaafsma 1980; Whitley 2011). Others have argued that the shamanic reading is applied too freely and rests on thin ethnographic ground (Quinlan 2000). Neither reading is definitive.

### Fremont Rock Art

Fremont rock art, associated with the Fremont cultural period of roughly AD 300–1300 across Utah (with most Emery County panels assigned to the later part of that span), represents a shift toward petroglyphic expression (Simms and Gohier 2010). Fremont sites in Emery County are often located in open-air settings, on canyon walls, and near water. Characteristic Fremont anthropomorphs have trapezoidal bodies, broad shoulders, headdresses or elaborate crowns, necklaces, and shield-like ornaments; many carry objects interpreted as weapons or ceremonial items. The style is energetic and narrative: panels frequently show bighorn sheep, deer, hunting scenes, and what appears to be a record of social and ceremonial life (Schaafsma 1971, 1980; Simms and Gohier 2010).

The Rochester Panel — the Rochester–Muddy Creek Petroglyph Site, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975 — stands on a promontory above the confluence of Rochester Creek and Muddy Creek east of Moore, in the southern part of the county (NRHP, NRIS 75001803; BLM, Rochester Panel). It is one of the most densely packed Fremont petroglyph assemblages in the region: dozens of animal and human figures, a rainbow-like arc, and geometric forms pecked into a single dark-varnished face, with stylistic evidence of Barrier Canyon–era, Fremont, and later additions layered on one another (Ancient Art Archive, Rochester; Castleton 1978–79). Temple Mountain Wash and other Swell drainages contain further Fremont petroglyphs with clear, deeply pecked human figures and mountain sheep (Castleton 1978–79).

### Ute Rock Art

Ute rock art production in the region became more visible after roughly the 16th century, coinciding with Ute occupation of the Colorado Plateau margins and, later, the adoption of horses introduced by Spanish colonists. (Numic-speaking ancestors of the Ute had moved into the Great Basin centuries earlier; the 16th-century date refers to identifiable historic-period rock art, not to the Numic arrival itself.) Ute images often incorporate narrative content — horses, riders, bison, bears, and scenes of hunting or conflict — rendered with less geometric elaboration than Fremont work and a greater emphasis on recognizable, sometimes datable, subject matter (Cole 2009; Patterson 2015). Bears in particular carry deep significance in Ute ceremonial life, and their appearance in panels has been read in that light.

The late Clifford Duncan (1933–2014), a Northern Ute spiritual elder and longtime cultural consultant from Fort Duchesne, argued that rock art should be understood not as archaeological artifacts but as living records of spiritual and historical experience, and that any interpretation must involve dialogue with Ute knowledge keepers. His readings of petroglyphs in western Colorado and on the Uintah and Ouray Reservation, recorded with anthropologist Carol Patterson, are the most fully documented Ute-perspective interpretation of regional rock art in print (Patterson and Duncan 2014; Patterson 2015).

## 12.2 Symbolic Motifs & Iconography

The recurring symbols across Emery County's panels offer clues to shared meanings and cultural values, though interpretation requires caution and deference to Indigenous perspectives.

**Anthropomorphic Figures:** The human form — whether a slender Barrier Canyon spirit-being or a heavily ornamented Fremont figure — appears in nearly every major panel, usually in a position of visual prominence. The elongated BCS bodies have been read as depictions of altered perception or spirit-world beings, while the elaborate dress and equipment of Fremont anthropomorphs suggest records of status, ritual role, or leadership (Schaafsma 1980; Simms and Gohier 2010).

**Mountain Sheep (Bighorn):** Bighorn sheep occur with high frequency in Fremont panels and also appear in Barrier Canyon compositions, often with exaggerated, curling horns. Because mountain sheep were both a major subsistence resource and a spiritually charged animal across the Great Basin, these images probably carried meaning beyond simple hunting tallies (Whitley 2011). In some Fremont panels, humans and sheep interact in compositions that may depict hunts or ritual relationships with game.

**Spirals and Geometric Patterns:** Concentric circles, spirals, zigzags, and grids appear throughout the county's rock art. They may represent water, travel, astronomical phenomena, or abstract spiritual concepts. Without Indigenous oral traditions directly linked to specific panels, interpretation remains speculative, though comparisons with Puebloan iconography and ethnography suggest that spirals in particular may relate to water, emergence, migration, or cyclical time (Schaafsma 1980; Cole 2009).

**Shields and Ornaments:** Fremont panels frequently show figures with circular shield-like forms, necklaces, headdresses, and other regalia. The care with which these objects are rendered suggests they conveyed status, protection, or ritual identity within Fremont communities (Simms and Gohier 2010).

**Bear Imagery:** Bears and bear paws appear in Ute and some earlier contexts. In Ute tradition the bear is associated with healing, protection, and strength, and bear imagery has been connected by Ute interpreters to ceremony and to places of power (Patterson 2015).

## 12.3 Chronology & Dating Techniques

Establishing the age of rock art is one of the hardest problems in archaeology. Emery County's panels have been dated, directly and indirectly, by several complementary methods.

**Radiocarbon Dating of Pigments:** Organic carbon in paint — charcoal, or a plant or animal binder — can be isolated by selective plasma oxidation and dated by accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS), a technique introduced for rock paintings in 1990 (Russ et al. 1990). Direct dates on Barrier Canyon pigments have been few and have varied widely, which is one reason the style's age remains contested. Charcoal can also carry an "old-wood" problem, in which the wood burned was already old when the paint was made, so such dates are best read as maximum ages or as members of a distribution rather than precise calendar years (Whitley 2011).

**Luminescence Dating:** Optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) measures when sediment grains were last exposed to sunlight. Applied to the Great Gallery in Horseshoe Canyon, OSL dating of rockfall debris and of the buried canyon floor showed that the painted wall could not have been available before roughly AD 1 and that the paintings predate about AD 1100, narrowing that panel's window to the end of the Archaic and the Fremont era (Pederson et al. 2014). The method dates the surface, not the paint, and its application to the Great Gallery has been disputed (Simon and Reed 2014; Pederson et al. 2015).

**Patination and Weathering:** Petroglyphs are pecked through the dark mineral coating known as rock varnish. Over centuries the exposed lighter rock re-darkens, so heavily re-varnished petroglyphs are generally older than those that remain bright. The method gives relative order, not calendar dates (Whitley 2011).

**Superposition:** Where images overlap, the sequence of cultures can be read directly. At the Rochester Panel and elsewhere, later figures are pecked over or beside earlier ones, providing a stratigraphy of cultural succession spanning many centuries (Castleton 1978–79; Schaafsma 1980).

**Cultural Context:** Datable features in the art itself — bows and arrows, horses, specific headdress forms — and association with dated habitation sites, ceramics, and architecture provide supporting frameworks. Horses, for example, mark any panel as post-contact, while certain Fremont regalia correlate with ceramic and architectural sequences (Schaafsma 1980; Simms and Gohier 2010).

## 12.4 Pigments & Lithic Sources

The pigments used in Emery County rock art were drawn from locally available minerals, reflecting practical knowledge and long-standing tradition.

**Red Ochre:** The dominant pigment in Barrier Canyon Style and many other panels is red ochre — iron oxide, chiefly hematite (Fe₂O₃). The red beds exposed across the San Rafael Swell, including the Moenkopi and Chinle formations, are rich in iron oxides, making the raw material readily accessible. Ochre was ground to powder and mixed with a binder to produce paint applied by finger, brush, or blown through a tube (Schaafsma 1980).

**Yellow, Brown, and White:** Limonite and goethite supply yellow and brown; white pigments were made from kaolin clay, gypsum, or calcite, all of which occur in the Swell's sedimentary sequence.

**Black:** Black elements come from charcoal, manganese oxides, or magnetite. Charcoal was the most accessible.

**Pigment Analysis:** Non-destructive portable X-ray fluorescence (pXRF) has been used across the West to characterize rock-art pigments without sampling, and such work generally confirms the use of local iron and manganese minerals (Newman and Loendorf 2005). No published pXRF study specific to Emery County panels was located in preparing this chapter; site-specific pigment provenance remains a research gap.

**Binding Agents:** The media used to bind pigment and adhere it to rock are poorly understood. Ethnographic accounts and experimental archaeology point to animal fats, plant resins, egg, blood, or saliva. The persistence of some panels across millennia, despite weathering and ultraviolet exposure, suggests effective binders, but the recipes are unknown and probably varied (Whitley 2011).

## 12.5 Site Distribution & Spatial Analysis

Emery County's rock art is not randomly distributed. Documented panels — numbering in the hundreds, though no complete public inventory exists — cluster in particular drainages, landforms, and ecotones, reflecting intentional placement and cultural values about meaningful places (Castleton 1978–79; URARA).

**San Rafael Swell Concentration:** The highest density of sites lies within and along the margins of the San Rafael Swell, notably Buckhorn Wash, Black Dragon Canyon, Temple Mountain Wash, and the San Rafael River canyons. This clustering likely reflects the Swell's water, game, and plant resources and its dramatic, visually prominent canyon walls and sheltered alcoves, which drew Archaic, Fremont, and Ute peoples alike (Schaafsma 1980; Cole 2009).

**Water Source Proximity:** Many panels stand near springs, seeps, and stream confluences, suggesting that rock art was created where resources converged, where people gathered, or where water itself held spiritual significance. The Rochester Panel's position above the meeting of Rochester Creek and Muddy Creek is typical (BLM, Rochester Panel).

**Elevation and Ecological Zones:** Sites span the county's elevational range, from the low desert canyons of the San Rafael River to the margins of the high plateaus, reflecting the seasonal movement of Archaic and Fremont peoples through multiple ecological zones (Schaafsma 1980).

**Visibility and Landscape Significance:** Some panels occupy visually prominent positions — cliff faces above travel corridors, canyon confluences, high viewpoints — suggesting that visibility was a deliberate criterion. Others are hidden, accessible only to those who know where to look, suggesting possible restricted or sacred functions (Whitley 2011).

## 12.6 Landscape Context & View-Shed Analysis

Rock art panels are embedded in broader landscapes whose visual, ecological, and spiritual dimensions shaped their creation and meaning.

**View-Sheds and Visual Prominence:** Analysis of the views *from* rock art sites and the visibility *of* sites from the surrounding landscape reveals intentional relationships with the environment. Some panels are visible from considerable distances and may have served communicative or territorial functions; others are visible only from a nearby water source or trail, suggesting specialized audiences (Whitley 2011).

**Canyon Corridor Networks:** The Swell's major drainages — Buckhorn Wash, Black Dragon Canyon, Temple Mountain Wash — function as corridors along which panels recur. These networks may trace traditional travel routes, seasonal rounds, or ritual pathways maintained across generations (Schaafsma 1980; Cole 2009).

**Astronomical Alignment:** At some Southwestern sites, light-and-shadow interactions with rock art mark solstices or other solar events (Malville and Putnam 1989). No systematic archaeoastronomical study specific to Emery County panels was located in preparing this chapter (a 2025 regional study of Archaic petroglyph sites in east-central Utah is noted in Chapter 7), and claims of alignments at specific county sites should be treated as unverified.

**Sacred Landscape Continuity:** For contemporary Ute, Paiute, Shoshone, and Puebloan communities, the Emery County landscape — including its rock art — remains part of living sacred geographies. Places marked by rock art often correspond to locations identified in oral tradition and practice, a continuity that archaeological interpretation alone cannot capture (Stoffle, Halmo, and Austin 1997; Patterson and Duncan 2014).

## 12.7 Sacred Landscapes & Oral Traditions

The rock art of Emery County is inseparable from the oral traditions, spiritual practices, and landscape meanings that Indigenous communities continue to maintain and transmit.

**Vision Quest Sites and Places of Power:** Ethnographic and archaeological evidence from across the Great Basin and far West indicates that some rock art was made at places associated with vision seeking, spiritual retreat, or concentrated supernatural power (Whitley 2011). The Barrier Canyon figures, with postures suggesting altered states or spiritual presence, have been proposed as markers of such places — a reading that is plausible but not demonstrated (Schaafsma 1980; Quinlan 2000).

**Ceremonial and Gathering Places:** Some concentrations of rock art coincide with evidence of repeated gatherings or seasonal occupation. Major compositions such as the Rochester Panel may mark places where communities convened, with the art serving to commemorate, announce, or sanctify those places.

**Ute Oral Histories and Sacred Geographies:** Ute elders and cultural knowledge keepers identify sites across the region as significant in Ute spiritual and historical narrative. Clifford Duncan's interpretations emphasize that rock art belongs to a living landscape narrative, not a closed archaeological record; that some places mark events, the presence of spirits, or concentrations of power; and that such understandings are grounded in Ute knowledge and practice rather than in anything archaeology can verify. They deserve primary consideration in any respectful engagement with the sites (Patterson and Duncan 2014; Patterson 2015).

**Fremont and Puebloan Continuities:** Scholars and descendant communities have proposed continuities between the Fremont and later Puebloan peoples. If such links exist, Hopi, Zuni, and other Puebloan traditions of emergence and migration may illuminate aspects of Fremont imagery. Collaborative research with descendant communities is the appropriate path to such connections, and it must respect the intellectual and spiritual property of Puebloan knowledge systems (Colwell-Chanthaphonh and Ferguson 2008).

**Pseudoarchaeology and Respectful Interpretation:** Emery County rock art has occasionally been subjected to speculative claims — extraterrestrial visitors, lost civilizations, non-Native creators. No archaeological evidence supports such claims, and they erase the agency and achievement of the Indigenous peoples who made the art (Feder 2020). Responsible interpretation acknowledges that many meanings may be unknown to non-Native researchers and that Indigenous communities are the appropriate authorities on cultural meaning and spiritual significance.

## 12.8 Preservation & Conservation Methods

Emery County's rock art faces threats from vandalism, weathering, climate change, energy development, and recreational use. Preservation draws on several strategies.

**Buckhorn Wash Panel: A Case Study in Restoration:** The Buckhorn Wash pictograph panel, a Barrier Canyon Style composition more than 130 feet long, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980 as part of the Buckhorn Wash Rock Art Sites district (NRHP, NRIS 80003898). By the early 1990s it had suffered decades of accumulated damage — chalking, charcoal and paint graffiti, and bullet impacts. In the summer of 1995 the Emery County Centennial Commission, working with the Bureau of Land Management, sponsored a professional conservation project costing roughly $110,000 that removed the modern graffiti and stabilized the surface; the cleaned panel was unveiled on September 23, 1995, as the county's statehood-centennial project (Deseret News, 3 Sept. 1995; BLM, Buckhorn Panel). Emery County hired New York art conservator Constance S. Silver for the work (Deseret News, "Centennial Legacies," September 29, 1996); she also treated the Sego Canyon panel in Grand County. The restoration demonstrates both the vulnerability of exposed panels and the possibility of careful, reversible intervention.

**Digital Documentation:** High-resolution photogrammetry and 3D scanning create precise digital records of panel surfaces, pigment, and weathering. Such records support research and management and reduce the need for repeated physical contact. Volunteer and agency documentation of Emery County panels is ongoing, though no county-wide scanning program has been published.

**Dust and Energy Development:** Disturbed soils on the Colorado Plateau are a significant source of wind-blown dust (Flagg et al. 2014), and dust settling on painted surfaces can obscure and abrade pigment. Rock-art–specific studies of this threat are lacking for Emery County, and monitoring of panels near roads and development is warranted.

**Visitor Management:** Growing recreational use of the San Rafael Swell has increased pressure on rock art sites. Interpretive signage, defined trails and viewing platforms, and regular patrols — the model now in place at Buckhorn Wash — are the standard tools for reducing damage (BLM, Buckhorn Panel; Whitley 2011). Balancing access with protection remains an ongoing challenge.

**Climate and Weathering:** Changing precipitation, temperature extremes, and ultraviolet exposure raise concern about accelerated pigment loss and surface exfoliation. Long-term condition monitoring and research into protective measures are conservation priorities (Whitley 2011).

**Legal Protection:** The Archaeological Resources Protection Act of 1979 makes it a federal crime to damage or remove archaeological resources, including rock art, on federal land, and the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990 and Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act require consultation with tribes on federal undertakings affecting cultural resources (16 U.S.C. § 470aa et seq.; 36 C.F.R. pt. 800).

**Collaborative Stewardship with Tribal Nations:** The most effective preservation involves tribal communities as active partners in management, research, and decision-making. The Bureau of Land Management's tribal-relations policy directs its field offices to consult with tribes on actions affecting places of cultural and religious importance (BLM Handbook H-1780-1, 2016), and the Ute Indian Tribe and other nations have increasingly taken leadership roles in documenting, protecting, and interpreting rock art within their ancestral territories.

## 12.9 Ethical Interpretation & Collaborative Stewardship

The future of rock art research and interpretation in Emery County lies in partnerships among archaeologists, land managers, and Indigenous communities, guided by respect, reciprocity, and recognition of Native sovereignty.

**Archaeological Limits and Indigenous Authority:** Non-Native archaeologists can document styles, dates, materials, and spatial patterns, but they cannot definitively interpret meanings that live in oral tradition, ceremony, or cultural knowledge held by descendant communities. Responsible practice acknowledges those limits and recognizes Indigenous peoples — not outside experts — as the primary authorities on cultural significance (Atalay 2012).

**Rejecting Pseudoarchaeology:** Speculative attributions of rock art to extraterrestrials, lost civilizations, or non-Native peoples should be rejected. They undermine Indigenous agency and distract from engagement with the cultures that created the art (Feder 2020).

**Protecting Sacred Sites and Sensitive Information:** Some locations hold particular significance for Ute, Paiute, Shoshone, or Puebloan communities and may be inappropriate for public visitation or detailed publication. Both Emery County National Register rock art listings are address-restricted for this reason (NRHP, NRIS 80003898, 75001803). Tribal consultation should precede research, publication, or management decisions affecting such places, and information held as sacred should not be published without tribal permission.

**Community-Based Research:** Models in which tribal members serve as co-investigators, knowledge holders, and decision-makers are becoming standard, combining archaeological and ethnographic methods with Indigenous knowledge to produce fuller and more respectful interpretations (Atalay 2012; Colwell-Chanthaphonh and Ferguson 2008).

**Publication and Attribution:** When research is published, tribal collaborators should be properly credited and compensated, and intellectual-property agreements should protect tribal knowledge while permitting collaborative publication.

**A Note on This Chapter's Review:** Because it addresses matters of profound significance to contemporary Ute, Paiute, Shoshone, and Puebloan communities, this chapter was flagged at drafting for review by tribal cultural representatives. That review was not obtained; in August 2026 the project's editor made the decision to publish without it rather than hold the chapter indefinitely. Readers should therefore understand §12.7 and §12.9 as the work of non-Native authors drawing on published sources, not as statements endorsed by any tribe. The editors welcome corrections, objections, or requests to remove material from the Ute Indian Tribe's cultural resources office and from any affected community, and commit to incorporating them in future editions.
