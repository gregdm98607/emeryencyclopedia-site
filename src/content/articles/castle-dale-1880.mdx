## A Stranger at the Door

Picture it: mid-June 1880, Castle Dale, Utah Territory. A man named L. D. Ensign stands at a doorway with a census book and a pencil. Whoever answers is about to tell a near-stranger their age, where they were born, where their mother and father were born, and what they do all day.

We hardly notice how odd that is. A government sends one man, house by house, to turn a town into rows and columns. Families answer him in the doorway, and their answers sit on paper for 146 years. The census is a ritual of its own: a once-a-decade handshake between a distant nation and a valley that had no railroad, no telegraph and, a year earlier, no town at all.

The [1880 federal census](/data/castle-dale-1880) counted **264 people in 50 houses**. That number is where most stories stop. This one starts there, because the way those 264 people grouped themselves tells us how Castle Dale actually worked.

## Before the Count

The settlers of 1879 did not arrive in an empty valley. When newcomers first reached this corner of the Colorado Plateau, it lay within the Ute domain. The San Pitch band used Castle Valley season by season, and the explorer John Wesley Powell understood the valley's very name as a translation of a Ute phrase meaning "stone house land." The census did not count Ute people in Castle Dale, and their absence from the page is part of the story too.

The newcomers moved fast. In 1879 the first families camped by the creek. In February 1880 the territorial legislature made Castle Dale the seat of the new Emery County. By June it was an official government town of fifty households, many still finishing their houses.

## A Town Assembled From Elsewhere

Almost nobody in Castle Dale was from Castle Dale. Ensign's birthplace column reads like a map of the world the town was built from:

[Thirty-seven people were born in Denmark](/data/castle-dale-1880?birthPlace=Denmark), the largest group. There were thirteen English, five Swedes, five Scots and one Welshwoman. Seven Americans came from the South: Mississippi, North Carolina, Texas, Virginia and Louisiana. A little girl, Louisa Anderson, was born in Arizona Territory, and a little boy, John V. Burdick, in Nevada. Dozens of the youngest were born in Utah.

Why would Danish farmers, Scottish stonemasons and a Louisiana-born woman end up in the same small valley? The answer is a single network. Latter-day Saint missionaries had worked in Scandinavia since the 1850s, and converts answered the call to *gather*, the church's word for leaving home to build a community of believers in the West. Faith decided not only what people believed but where they lived, whom they lived beside, and who would help raise their roof.

## Work as a Web

Most men told Ensign they farmed. The other trades show a town building itself out of its own hands. Two stonemasons, both Scottish, and a carpenter, John K. Reid, were the people who turned tents into houses. Joseph Boulden was the blacksmith, and Aldrin Burdick the harness maker, the men who kept plows and horses working. Hannah Lawson, a Swede, worked as a servant in another family's home. Thomas Farrer ranched, and told Ensign he was divorced, a rare answer in Utah in 1880.

Read together, the list is less a job directory than a web of dependence. The farmer needed the blacksmith, and the blacksmith needed the farmer's grain. Reid himself was Scottish-born to Irish parents and married to an Englishwoman, so even one household could hold three countries.

One trade we cannot read. Next to Samuel Stowe's name Ensign wrote something like *Stoneman*, or *Horner*, or *Stovemaker*. The record keeps its secret.

## Reading the Households

An anthropologist's unit of study is often not the person but the household: who eats together, who is missing, whose names repeat. Castle Dale's fifty households hold seven puzzles worth slowing down for.

### 1. The house that isn't on the page

Ensign numbered houses as he walked. One, two, three, up through seven, then nine. There is no dwelling 8 anywhere in Castle Dale's pages. Maybe a slip of the pencil. Maybe an empty cabin, or a family that was out in the fields. The gap is a reminder that a census records what one man saw on one walk, not everything that was there.

### 2. Two living sons named James

The O'Fallons of dwelling 50, the last household counted, had four boys: [James (11), Jacob (8), James E. (5) and Louis (3)](/data/castle-dale-1880?q=o%27fallon). Two Jameses, both alive, at one table.

Naming carries meaning. In many nineteenth-century families, when a child died, the next baby could receive the same name, keeping it alive in the family. The six-year gap between the two Jameses leaves room for a lost child in between. That is a hypothesis, not a finding. Burial and church records could settle it.

### 3. A married man living without his wife

In dwelling 38, Grett Burnett, 68, born in New York, told Ensign he was married. His son Joseph, 26, lived with him. No wife appears. She may have been visiting family, ill elsewhere, recently dead, or living with another of their children. The 1880 form had no column for "away," so the household shows us its shape and hides its reason.

### 4. Two birthplaces for one little girl

Dwelling 14 held Andrew Andersen, born in Sweden, his wife Dyantha, born in Denmark, and two little girls. Ensign wrote that the older girl, also Dyantha, two, was born in Denmark, and that baby Christee was born in Utah. The family's own church record tells it another way. Andrew and Dyantha married in Salt Lake City in January 1875, and the Orangeville Ward record, as copied onto the family group sheet, gives the older girl's birth as June 1877 at Fountain Green, in Sanpete County. So the ocean crossings came before the wedding, not with a toddler in arms. A census is one answer given at one doorway. Here it disagrees with the record the family's ward kept, and the ward record is the likelier of the two.

### 5. A Louisiana-born wife in a plural household

Dwelling 18 was the household of Erastus Curtis: thirteen children and two wives. Mary C. was born in Delaware; Joanna, his plural wife, in Louisiana. The federal form was built for one kind of family. What every neighbor in Castle Dale understood about the Curtis home, the census had no way to say. How Joanna came to the faith, and to this valley, is a story we have not yet found.

### 6. A mother at sixteen

In dwelling 21, Mary E. Guymon, 16, was married to James, 29, and mother to William, 1. She married at fourteen or fifteen. That was young even then. Utah Territory passed no marriage law at all until 1888; until then its courts followed the old common law, which set the bar well below her age. The census shows her age. It cannot tell us how she saw her own life, and her voice, if it survives anywhere, would be in a diary or a family memory.

### 7. One word in an empty column

The health column was usually blank. Next to Cena Swasey, 22, born in Iowa to Danish parents and married to Charles, a California-born rancher in dwelling 42, Ensign wrote one word: *Consumption.* That was the century's name for tuberculosis, the disease Americans feared most. He wrote the word and walked on to dwelling 43. Later records may tell us what happened to her.

## The Pattern Beneath the Puzzles

Put the households side by side and Castle Dale's logic shows. Kinship, faith and land were not separate parts of life; each explained the others. Two of the fifty households were openly plural: the Curtises, and the Bagleys, whose two wives and nine children lived one dwelling apart on the same street. Families like the Seeleys of dwelling 15 would soon draw brothers in from Sanpete County, and a single family name could anchor a neighborhood for generations.

The census also captured a town about to change. About forty young people aged 12 to 18 lived in Castle Dale that June. Whether the founders' children stayed is one of the big questions about every new Western town.

## What We Still Want to Know

The next good look at Castle Dale is 1900, because most of the 1890 federal census was lost after a 1921 fire in Washington, D.C. Twenty years is a long silence. Across it, we want to learn:

**Who stayed?** How many of the 264 still lived in Castle Dale in 1900? **What became of the plural households** after the 1890 Manifesto ended new plural marriages? **Who died young,** and who arrived? Castle Dale more than doubled by 1900; did the Danes keep coming, or did someone new? We'll follow the Seeleys, the Andersens of dwelling 13, the Wilsons of dwelling 40 (the largest household), and the Biddlecomes of dwelling 31, whose middle child carries a name we are still trying to read.

## Here Ends Castle Dale

On his last page, L. D. Ensign wrote three words: *Here Ends Castle Dale.* For him, the town was finished. It had been counted.

For the people inside those columns, the census was one interruption in a June full of water to haul, fields to plant and walls to raise. They had their own words for who they were: neighbors, kin, a ward, a people gathered.

*Ensign's book ended. Their town was just beginning.*
