## A Piece of Paper From Washington

When the first families reached Castle Valley, the ground they plowed did not belong to them. It belonged to the United States. A settler could build a cabin, dig a ditch and raise a crop, and still own none of it on paper.

The paper that changed that was called a **patent**. A land patent is the deed by which the federal government hands a piece of public land to its first private owner. Each one names the person, the date, and exactly which squares of ground were granted.

Castle Dale was founded in 1879. The earliest patent in this study is dated 10 March 1883. Between that day and 4 August 1920, the government issued **171 patents** in the two townships around Castle Dale and Orangeville. Together they cover 21,414 acres, a little more than 33 square miles.

We have read every patent that has an image; a few survive only as index entries. [The full table is here](/data/castle-dale-land), and each row links to the record at the Bureau of Land Management.

## Four Ways to Get Land

The patents fall into four kinds, each from a different law.

- **Homestead entries: 105.** Under the Homestead Act of 1862, a settler could claim up to 160 acres, live on it, improve it, and receive title after five years.
- **Cash sales: 35.** Under an 1820 law, public land could simply be bought, at a minimum of $1.25 an acre.
- **Desert land entries: 27.** The Desert Land Act of 1877 offered dry land to anyone who would bring water to it.
- **Timber culture entries: 4.** A settler could earn land by planting trees on part of it.

About six patents in ten were for a quarter section, 160 acres. The smallest were 40 acres, a square a quarter mile on each side.

## One Day in October

The first patent went to James G. Jack on 10 March 1883: a cash purchase of 160.7 acres on the Orangeville side. On 10 May 1884, Niels P. Miller and James C. Jensen received the first two homestead patents, both reaching into Section 33, where the town stands.

Then came 18 October 1886. On that single day the land office issued **thirteen** Castle Dale patents, more than on any other day in the record. The names read like a roll call of the early valley: John K. Reid and Henry Reid, Orange Seely and Justus W. Seely, John C. Snow and Samuel P. Snow, Edwin M. Cox, Frederick Andersen, Andrew C. Jensen, Andrew Rasmussen, Charles A. Moffitt, William B. Curtis and Edward Smith. All thirteen were homesteads, twelve of about 160 acres and one of 120. Together they came to 2,044 acres.

A homestead took at least five years to earn. Count back from 1886 and you reach 1881 or earlier, within two years of the town's founding. These were people who had come early and stayed.

## The Land Fills In

Here is how fast the valley passed into private hands:

| Patented by | Patents | Running total |
|---|---|---|
| 1890 | 33 | 33 |
| 1900 | 59 | 92 |
| 1910 | 40 | 132 |
| 1920 | 39 | 171 |

The busiest ten years were the 1890s. The kind of patent changed over time, too. Homesteads dominate the early years. Desert land entries come in two waves, from 1893 to 1901 and from 1917 to 1920. In 1911 and 1912 there were ten cash sales in two years, most of them about 80 acres or less. The last patent in the study, to Archibald G. Livingston on 4 August 1920, was a 40-acre desert land entry.

Public land lasted longer than you might guess. On 19 February 1912, Alfred D. Dickson bought 120 acres from the government that touched Section 34, one of the two sections the town was platted on. Thirty-three years after Castle Dale was founded, there was still federal land at its edge.

## What Is Under the Town?

Castle Dale's streets and blocks were surveyed in December 1880. You might expect the town to have its own patent. In the records read so far, it does not.

Lay the 1880 plat over the patent map and almost all of it falls inside land patented to individuals. As nearly as an 1880 plat can be fitted to modern survey lines, five patents cover about 97 percent of the ground the plat takes in: James M. Peterson (1887), Christian G. Larsen (1886), Niels P. Miller (1884), Carl Wilberg (1891) and Andrew E. Smith (1895).

What the patents do not say is how the town lots then passed to the families who lived on them. That answer is in the county's deed books, which this study has not yet read. For now it is an open question, and a good one.

## What a Patent Cannot Tell You

A patent is a starting point. It records the day land left the public domain, and nothing after. Land was sold, divided, inherited and lost, and none of that appears here. So this is a map of who got the land first. It is not a map of who owned it in 1900 or 1920.

The government's index is not perfect either. It leaves out one legal description, twice lists the man who first filed alongside the buyer who actually received the patent, and labels seventeen desert land entries as cash sales. Where the patent image and the index disagree, we followed the image.
