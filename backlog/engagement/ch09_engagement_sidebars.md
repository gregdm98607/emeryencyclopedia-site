## SIDEBAR 1: Factbox — "Fremont Ceramic Types in the San Rafael Region"

**Title**: "Fremont Ceramic Types in the San Rafael Region"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 9.2 (Ceramic Traditions & Basketry Techniques)

**Text**:
> **FREMONT CERAMIC TYPES IN THE SAN RAFAEL REGION**
> | Type | Temper material | Surface treatment | Primary use |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Emery Gray | Sand, calcite | Plain or lightly smoothed | Cooking and storage |
> | Snake Valley Gray | Crushed quartzite | Plain, occasionally incised | Cooking and storage |
> | Uinta Gray | Sand or grit | Corrugated or neck-banded | Cooking |
> | Turner Gray | Calcite | Neck-banded, plain body | Storage and transport |
>
> All four types are utilitarian graywares built by the coil-and-scrape method. The relative plainness of Fremont ceramics compared to Ancestral Puebloan painted wares reflects different cultural priorities: the Fremont invested their artistic energy in rock art.

**Purpose**: Converts the dense paragraph-form ceramic typology in §9.2 into a scannable comparison table; helps readers distinguish the four main types without re-reading prose.

## SIDEBAR 2: FamilyActivity — "Build a Fremont Pit-House Model"

**Title**: "Build a Fremont Pit-House Model"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 9.4 (Pit House & Village Architecture)

**Text**:
> **TRY THIS: Build a Fremont Pit-House Model**
> **What you need**: Damp clay or a tray of moist soil; small sticks or pencils (4–8 inches); dry grass, pine needles, or strips of bark; optional small stones for a central fire pit.
> **Time**: 1–2 hours | **Ages**: 7 and up
>
> 1. Press a shallow oval depression about 1 inch deep into damp clay or soil — this is the pit.
> 2. Stand six to eight small sticks upright around the rim as wall posts, spacing them evenly.
> 3. Lean longer sticks from the rim posts inward to a central point, creating a dome-shaped roof framework.
> 4. Weave dry grass or leaf strips through the framework, then press a thin layer of damp clay over the roof to simulate the earth-and-brush covering.
>
> **Discussion**: How does soil surrounding the pit walls insulate the interior compared to above-ground walls? How would a family of four or five people use this single room for sleeping, cooking, and storing food?

**Purpose**: Gives families a hands-on connection to the architecture described in §9.4; the discussion question draws a link between the Fremont design choice and basic thermal physics that children can grasp.

## SIDEBAR 3: TriviaCallout — "The Pilling Figurines: Stolen and Returned"

**Title**: "DID YOU KNOW? The Pilling Figurines Were Stolen — and Came Back"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 9.10 (Ritual & Ceremonial Practices)

**Text**:
> **DID YOU KNOW?**
> The eleven Pilling Figurines — unfired clay human figures discovered near Huntington Canyon in 1950 — are among the finest Fremont ritual objects ever recovered. They were stolen from the USU Eastern Prehistoric Museum in Price in 1994 and vanished for seventeen years before being anonymously returned in 2011. No one was ever charged. They are now on display at the College of Eastern Utah Prehistoric Museum in Price, where visitors can see exactly the kind of detailed hairstyles and neck ornaments depicted in the rock art panels of the San Rafael Swell.

**Purpose**: The theft-and-return story is genuinely dramatic and memorable; it anchors the abstract discussion of Fremont ritual objects to a specific, recoverable place (the Price museum) and a verifiable recent event. Directly follows the paragraph discussing clay figurines in §9.10.

## SIDEBAR 4: ScavengerHunt — "Fremont Rock Art Observer's Challenge"

**Title**: "Fremont Rock Art Observer's Challenge"

**Location in Chapter**: After Proposed Tables section (bottom of chapter)

**Text**:
> **SCAVENGER HUNT: Fremont Rock Art Observer's Challenge** — *Can you find all 5 at Buckhorn Wash?*
>
> Buckhorn Wash Pictograph Panel, San Rafael Swell — BLM road off SR-10, east of Castle Dale. Stay behind viewing barriers; never touch the rock surface.
>
> 1. [ ] A trapezoidal-bodied anthropomorph — broad shoulders, narrow waist, rectangular torso. This is the classic Fremont body form.
> 2. [ ] A figure wearing a headdress, earrings, or necklace — look for lines, arcs, or dots extending above the head or along the shoulder line.
> 3. [ ] A panel where dark desert varnish has been pecked away to reveal lighter rock beneath — compare the exposed surface color to the surrounding varnish.
> 4. [ ] A bighorn sheep petroglyph — note the backward-curved horns and the stylized legs.
> 5. [ ] A place where red painted pictographs and pecked petroglyphs appear on the same rock surface — evidence of two traditions and possibly two eras.
>
> **Bonus challenge**: Find a figure that could be interpreted as both a human and an animal — scholars still debate the meaning of hybrid figures in Fremont rock art.
>
> *All Fremont rock art sites are protected under federal law. Report vandalism to the BLM Price Field Office: 435-636-3600.*

**Purpose**: Directs readers to the main public-access site (Buckhorn Wash, which has maintained viewing barriers and BLM infrastructure). All items are observable from the designated viewing area without approaching the panel. Includes legal/protocol note in-item.

## SIDEBAR 5: PhotoAssignment — "Photograph a Fremont Granary"

**Title**: "Photograph a Fremont Granary"

**Location in Chapter**: After Proposed Tables section (bottom of chapter)

**Text**:
> **PHOTO CHALLENGE: Photograph a Fremont Granary**
> **Your subject**: A Fremont-era stone granary or storage cist built into a sandstone alcove
> **Where**: Several granaries are visible from public roads along Buckhorn Wash and Nine Mile Canyon — check BLM interpretive signs for exact locations; never approach or climb toward the structures
> **What to look for**: Shoot from a distance that shows the alcove framing the structure — the relationship between the granary and the overhang above is the story. Avoid close telephoto compression; a moderate zoom showing the whole alcove context works best.
> **The story you're telling**: The Fremont separated living space on the valley floor from food storage high in the cliff face — a deliberate design choice protecting supplies from flooding, rodents, and raiders. A granary photographed in its alcove tells that story at a glance.
> *Share your photo with #EmeryEncyclopedia*

**Purpose**: Connects the storage architecture described in §9.4 to a visible, photographable field subject; the framing instruction teaches basic documentary photography composition while reinforcing the architectural concept.

## SIDEBAR 6: FieldItinerary — "Buckhorn Draw Scenic Byway to the Wedge Overlook"

**Title**: "Buckhorn Draw Scenic Byway to the Wedge Overlook"

**Location in Chapter**: After Proposed Tables section (bottom of chapter)

**Text**:
> **FIELD GUIDE: Buckhorn Draw Scenic Byway to the Wedge Overlook**
> **Getting there**: From Castle Dale, drive north on SR-10, then east on the Green River Cutoff Road. Follow BLM signs for Buckhorn Draw. Unpaved but generally passable for standard vehicles in dry conditions; check with the BLM Price Field Office (435-636-3600) after rain or snow.
> **GPS**: Buckhorn Wash Pictograph Panel: 39.1631° N, 110.7422° W; Wedge Overlook: 39.1978° N, 110.6903° W
> **Time**: Half day, 4–5 hours including drive and stops | **Season**: March–May or September–November; avoid summer midday heat
> **Bring**: 2 liters of water per person, sunscreen, hat, sturdy footwear, binoculars for panel detail, paper or downloaded offline map (cell service unreliable in the Swell)
> **On the ground**: Begin at the Buckhorn Wash Pictograph Panel — one of the finest Barrier Canyon Style sites in the world. Stay behind the barrier fence and use binoculars for close detail; do not touch the pigments. Skin oils and pressure accelerate deterioration, and the panels are protected under federal law. Continue north to the Wedge Overlook for a panoramic view of the Little Grand Canyon of the San Rafael River — the canyon floor 1,000 feet below preserves the same seasonal drainages that supported Fremont farming villages.
> **See also**: Chapter 5 (San Rafael Swell Geology), Chapter 8 (Paleo-Indian and Archaic Periods), Chapter 12 (Rock Art Traditions)

**Purpose**: Gives families a complete, drivable half-day itinerary connecting the rock art discussion in §9.5 to an accessible, maintained public site. Includes BLM protocol note in the "on the ground" text per Part II indigenous content guidelines.
