## SIDEBAR 1: FamilyActivity — "Build a Mini Watershed"

**Title**: "Build a Mini Watershed"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 3.2 (Surface-Water Hydrography & Seasonal Flow Regimes)

**Text**:
> **TRY THIS: Build a Mini Watershed**
> **What you need**: Large baking sheet or shallow plastic bin, modeling clay or Play-Doh, spray bottle with blue-tinted water, flour or baking soda (for "snowpack"), pencil or straw to sketch canal routes
> **Time**: 30 min | **Ages**: 6+
> 1. Shape a miniature landscape: mountains on one end (the Wasatch Plateau), a valley in the middle (Castle Valley), and a low channel on the far side (the Green River).
> 2. Dust the mountain peaks with flour or baking soda to represent the snowpack.
> 3. Use the spray bottle to simulate spring melt. Watch where water flows, where it pools, and where it rushes.
> **Discussion**: Where would you build a dam to store water for the dry months? Where would a flash flood be most dangerous? This is the same gravity-driven system that shapes every farm, town, and canyon in Emery County.

**Purpose**: Gives children a tactile model of the snowmelt-dominant flow regime just introduced in 3.2 — makes drainage basins and seasonal flow concrete before the more technical sections that follow.

## SIDEBAR 2: Factbox — "What Irrigation Does to Water Quality"

**Title**: "What Irrigation Does to Water Quality"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 3.6 (Water Chemistry & Quality Indicators), after the 15,700-tons-per-year figure

**Text**:
> **WHAT IRRIGATION DOES TO WATER QUALITY**
> | Measurement | Value |
> |---|---|
> | Dissolved solids — water diverted from creeks | 357 mg/L |
> | Dissolved solids — water returning to Green River | 4,170 mg/L |
> | Concentration increase | 11.7× saltier |
> | Annual salt load to Green River (Green River area) | ~15,700 tons/year |
>
> **What does this mean?** Fifteen thousand tons of salt is roughly equivalent to filling 115 loaded coal cars — dissolved from ancient sea-floor rock and flushed into the Colorado River system every single year.

**Purpose**: Converts the 11.7× multiplier — the chapter's single most striking quantitative claim — into a visual table that readers can absorb at a glance rather than parsing out of a dense paragraph.

## SIDEBAR 3: TriviaCallout — "When the Canyon Fills Without Warning"

**Title**: "When the Canyon Fills Without Warning"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 3.7 (Flood History & Paleoflood Evidence)

**Text**:
> **DID YOU KNOW?**
> August is Utah's most dangerous flood month — about 35 percent of all recorded state flood events happen in August alone. In August 2021, a flash flood roared down Bear Canyon near the Gentry Mountain coal mine, sweeping a worker six miles downstream. In the slot canyons of the San Rafael Swell, a thunderstorm miles away can send a wall of water through a passage with perfectly clear skies overhead. There is no such thing as a false alarm when a flash flood warning reaches you in a canyon.

**Purpose**: The statistics about August being the peak flood month are compelling but abstract; the 2021 Bear Canyon event makes the risk visceral and county-specific. Placed immediately after 3.7 where both appear.

## SIDEBAR 4: ScavengerHunt — "The Water Detective Hunt"

**Title**: "The Water Detective Hunt"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 3.9 (Water Rights & Legal Framework) — placed here because the hunt covers infrastructure built up through the settlement/water-rights story just told

**Text**:
> **SCAVENGER HUNT: The Water Detective Hunt** — *Can you find all 4 items?*
> 1. [ ] At **Joes Valley Reservoir** — Find the dam face. The dam rises 187 feet above the streambed. Can you spot where Seely Creek enters the reservoir? Look for the muddy delta where sediment builds up.
> 2. [ ] Along **Huntington or Cottonwood Creek** — Find an irrigation headgate or canal entrance. Is water being diverted right now? Which direction does the canal run? Sketch the diversion structure in your notebook.
> 3. [ ] At the **Green River in Green River (town)** — Stand on the bridge and look west. Can you see where the San Rafael River enters? What color is the Green River water today — clear, green-brown, or muddy?
> 4. [ ] In any **San Rafael Swell slot canyon** (adult supervision required) — Find the highest water mark on the canyon wall. How far above the canyon floor does it reach? What debris — sticks, mud, rocks — tells you a flood has passed here?
>
> **Bonus challenge**: Visit waterdata.usgs.gov and look up station 09328500 (San Rafael River near Green River). Record today's discharge in cubic feet per second, then check again in a month.

**Purpose**: Turns the water infrastructure and flood hazard content into observable field tasks. Each item is anchored to a specific, visitable location mentioned in the chapter.

## SIDEBAR 5: TriviaCallout — "The Reservoir That Needs No Dam"

**Title**: "The Reservoir That Needs No Dam"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 3.11 (Climate Variability & Drought Impacts)

**Text**:
> **DID YOU KNOW?**
> More than 95 percent of Utah's water supply begins as snow. The Wasatch Plateau snowpack above Emery County functions like a frozen reservoir that accumulates from October through April at elevations above 9,000 feet — no dam, no pump, no infrastructure required. When it measured only 44 percent of normal in April 2025, Emery County entered a drought emergency. No engineered system can replace what the snowpack does for free every winter.

**Source**: Utah Division of Water Resources; Governor Spencer Cox drought declaration, April 2025

**Purpose**: The 95-percent statistic appears in 3.11 but can be easily skimmed; placing it in a TriviaCallout directly after the drought paragraph gives the drought emergency its context and makes the snowpack's irreplaceability memorable.

## SIDEBAR 6: FieldItinerary — "The Water Trail: Snowmelt to Salt"

**Title**: "The Water Trail: Snowmelt to Salt"

**Location in Chapter**: End of chapter, replacing the prose ## Engagement Features section

**Text**:
> **FIELD GUIDE: The Water Trail: Snowmelt to Salt**
> **Getting there**: Start on SR-31 in Huntington Canyon, drive through Castle Valley via US-6 and SR-10, cross I-70 at the San Rafael River, end at Green River State Park. ~120 miles.
> **Time**: Full day (6–8 hours with stops)
> **Season**: May–June for peak snowmelt and active canal flows; September–October for low-water San Rafael River
> **Bring**: Water and snacks (limited services in canyon areas), binoculars, a notebook for water observations
>
> **On the ground**:
> - **Stop 1 — Huntington Canyon above 8,000 ft**: In spring, snowmelt cascades directly off the plateau. This is where Emery County's water supply begins.
> - **Stop 2 — Huntington North Reservoir**: Compare the waterline to the high-water mark on the shore; in drought years the gap tells the whole story.
> - **Stop 3 — A Castle Valley irrigation canal**: Running through Huntington, Castle Dale, or Orangeville, these canals are snowmelt traveling to alfalfa fields.
> - **Stop 4 — San Rafael River at I-70**: The Mancos Shale badlands visible here are where water's dissolved solids jump 11.7× as it flows east toward the Green River.
> - **Stop 5 — Green River State Park**: Every drop that started in Huntington Canyon ends up here. If you started in the morning, you've followed a drop of water across 80 miles and 6,000 feet of elevation.
>
> **See also**: Chapter 2 (Geology), Chapter 4 (Climate & Weather)

**Purpose**: Gives readers a complete, drivable arc that connects every major concept in the chapter — snowpack, reservoirs, irrigation canals, salt loading, and the Green River. The five stops map directly to the chapter's five main themes.
