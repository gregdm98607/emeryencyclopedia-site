# Chapter 10 — Numic Expansion: Engagement Sidebars and Activities

**Indigenous content note:** Ch10 is a Part II chapter (Ch08–Ch13). All framing of Ute, Paiute, and Shoshone peoples uses present-tense constructions that affirm their existence as living nations. The FieldItinerary directs readers to a tribally-hosted public event (Northern Ute Powwow) rather than to archaeological sites. No scavenger hunt is included — the chapter's content is better served by activities emphasizing language and living culture.

---

## SIDEBAR 1: TriviaCallout — "One Language, a Continent of Range"

**Title**: "DID YOU KNOW? One Ancestral Language — Half a Continent of Range"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 10.1 (Proto-Numic Origins & Linguistic Evidence)

**Text**:
> **DID YOU KNOW?**
> All Numic languages — Ute, Southern Paiute, Shoshone, Comanche, and several others — descend from a single ancestral language spoken near Death Valley roughly 2,000 years ago. From that single origin, Numic-speaking peoples spread across one of the largest geographic footprints of any language family in pre-Columbian North America, from the Mojave Desert to Wyoming and from the California Sierra Nevada to the Great Plains. The Utes who called Castle Valley "Tompin-con-tu — rock house land" were part of this great linguistic diaspora.

**Source**: Lamb (1958); Fowler (1972)

**Purpose**: The scale of the Numic expansion is genuinely surprising and hard to visualize from prose alone; the trivia format anchors it to the chapter's opening linguistic evidence and connects to the Ute name for Castle Valley introduced in §10.2.

## SIDEBAR 2: FamilyActivity — "Numic Word-Walk"

**Title**: "Numic Word-Walk"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 10.5 (Seasonal Mobility & Resource Round)

**Text**:
> **TRY THIS: Numic Word-Walk**
> **What you need**: A printed or written word list; optional pocket notebook.
> **Time**: 30–60 minutes on a family walk | **Ages**: All ages
>
> Before your walk, visit the Ute Indian Tribe's website (utetribe.com) or the University of Utah's digital language resources to find five to ten Ute words for things you will encounter outdoors — canyon, river, mountain, spring, cottonwood, sage, deer, rock. Write them on a card to carry with you.
>
> As you walk, say each word aloud when you reach the thing it names.
>
> **Discussion**: How does learning even a few words in another language change how you pay attention to a place? Languages encode ways of knowing the land — what might a Ute word for a particular plant or landform reveal about how that resource was used?

**Purpose**: Connects the linguistic evidence in §10.1 and the seasonal round in §10.5 to a doable outdoor activity; emphasizes living Ute language as a present-day resource rather than a historical artifact. Using utetribe.com ensures readers engage with a tribally-controlled source.

## SIDEBAR 3: Factbox — "Major Ute Bands with Territory Near Emery County"

**Title**: "Major Ute Bands with Territory Near Emery County"

**Location in Chapter**: After Section 10.7 (Band Organization & Leadership Structures)

**Text**:
> **MAJOR UTE BANDS WITH TERRITORY NEAR EMERY COUNTY**
> | Band name | Core territory | Relationship to Emery County |
> |---|---|---|
> | San Pitch (Sanpits) | Sevier and Sanpete valleys, western Castle Valley | Primary seasonal users of Castle Valley |
> | Sheberetch | Eastern Utah, San Rafael Swell, Green River corridor | Seasonal use of southern and eastern Emery County |
> | Weeminuche | SE Utah, SW Colorado (Four Corners area) | Occasional use of southern desert margins |
> | Uintah | Uintah Basin, eastern Utah | Eastern Emery County and Green River approaches |
>
> Band territories were not rigid boundaries but overlapping seasonal ranges. All four bands are part of what became the Ute Indian Tribe of the Uintah and Ouray Reservation, the present-day governing body for Northern Ute peoples.

**Purpose**: The chapter's §10.2 discusses three bands by name but the prose is dense; the table format lets readers quickly identify each band's geographic relationship to Emery County. The closing note corrects a common misconception that these were separate tribes.

## SIDEBAR 4: FamilyActivity — "Ute Trade-Route Planner"

**Title**: "Ute Trade-Route Planner"

**Location in Chapter**: After Proposed Tables section (bottom of chapter)

**Text**:
> **TRY THIS: Ute Trade-Route Planner**
> **What you need**: A printed USGS topographic map of Emery County (free at apps.nationalmap.gov); colored pencils or markers; optional compass.
> **Time**: 30–60 minutes | **Ages**: 10 and up
>
> Using the map, design a travel route for a Ute family in September 1830, moving from a summer hunting camp on the high Wasatch Plateau (above 9,000 feet) to a winter camp in the protected canyon bottoms near the Green River (below 4,500 feet).
>
> Consider: Which passes would still be open in late September? Where would you find reliable water along the route? What food sources would be available — pinyon nuts, deer, rabbit? How many miles could a family on foot realistically cover per day?
>
> Mark your route, then discuss what you would carry and what you might cache for the return trip in spring.
>
> **Discussion**: How does a topographic map help you understand why the Ute seasonal round followed the pattern it did? What does the land itself teach you about the route?

**Purpose**: Translates the seasonal round described in §10.5 into a hands-on map-reading and spatial-reasoning activity; the 1830 framing is close enough to the pre-horse-to-horse transition era to prompt discussion of how technology changed mobility.

## SIDEBAR 5: PhotoAssignment — "Photograph a Pinyon-Juniper Woodland in Autumn"

**Title**: "Photograph a Pinyon-Juniper Woodland in Autumn"

**Location in Chapter**: After Proposed Tables section (bottom of chapter)

**Text**:
> **PHOTO CHALLENGE: Photograph a Pinyon-Juniper Woodland in Autumn**
> **Your subject**: A stand of pinyon pine (Pinus edulis) with visible cones, in October or November when cones are ripe and harvestable
> **Where**: The mid-elevation slopes above Castle Dale, along any unpaved road climbing toward the Wasatch Plateau between 6,000 and 7,500 feet
> **What to look for**: Look for trees with cones visible in the canopy or recently fallen. Photograph at low angle in morning or afternoon light to capture the texture of bark and the scale of the cones against your hand. A person in the frame gives scale.
> **The story you're telling**: The same pinyon groves you photograph have sustained human communities for at least 10,000 years — Archaic gatherers, Fremont farmers who supplemented their corn with wild foods, and Numic-speaking peoples who organized entire seasonal rounds around the autumn harvest. Pine nuts provided roughly 3,000 calories per pound and stored through winter. Your photograph connects you to that unbroken thread.
> *Share your photo with #EmeryEncyclopedia*

**Purpose**: Anchors the pinyon harvest discussion from §10.5 to a specific, doable autumn photography challenge; the calorie-density fact gives readers a concrete reason the harvest mattered so much.

## SIDEBAR 6: FieldItinerary — "Northern Ute Powwow, Fort Duchesne"

**Title**: "Northern Ute Powwow — Fort Duchesne, Uintah County"

**Location in Chapter**: After Proposed Tables section (bottom of chapter)

**Text**:
> **FIELD GUIDE: Northern Ute Powwow — Fort Duchesne, Uintah County**
> **Getting there**: From Castle Dale, drive north on US-6 through Price, then northeast on US-40 to Fort Duchesne (approximately 2.5 hours). The powwow grounds are on the Uintah and Ouray Reservation.
> **GPS**: Fort Duchesne, UT 84026
> **Time**: Full day, or camp overnight for multi-day events | **Season**: Summer, typically July or August — verify current dates at utetribe.com before traveling
> **Bring**: Cash for tribal vendors, respectful attire, willingness to listen and learn
> **On the ground**: The Northern Ute Powwow is a public event welcoming respectful visitors. Watch dancing and drumming from designated spectator areas. Purchase directly from tribal artisans — beadwork, jewelry, and traditional arts. Ask before photographing individuals; follow all posted event guidelines. The Ute people are a living nation with contemporary governance, language programs, and cultural institutions. This event is a reminder that the history in Chapter 10 continues today.
> **See also**: Chapter 11 (Spanish and Mexican Period), Chapter 15 (Settlement Era)

**Purpose**: Provides an experiential pathway for readers to engage with living Ute culture in a tribally-controlled, welcoming context. Deliberately chosen over an archaeological site to underscore that Numic peoples are present-day sovereign nations, not historical subjects.
